Abstract
Automatic short-answer grading (ASAG) can save teacher time. But graders that work well on familiar questions often do worse on new questions, and a grader that hands uncertain answers to a teacher must be judged on accuracy and coverage together. We study a three-signal scorer that labels student answers as Incorrect, Partial or Correct on two public English datasets, SciEntsBank and Beetle. The scorer averages a fine-tuned DistilRoBERTa classifier, SBERT embeddings with logistic regression, and natural-language-inference (NLI) features with logistic regression. It flags answers with confidence below 0.64 for teacher review. On a development set of 2,049 answers to 177 questions, the deployed scorer reached 56.4% accuracy and a quadratic weighted kappa (QWK) of 0.40. Accuracy was 69.2% on seen questions (150 questions) and 48.9% on unseen questions (27 questions). The review rule accepted about 31% of answers automatically, with 75.1% accuracy on those answers. The target of 85% accuracy at about 30% coverage was not met (grouped cross-validation estimate: 74.8% at 31.3%). Partial-credit answers cause the largest share of errors, and small hand-written checks show unreliable handling of negation. An alternative way of combining the outputs raised Partial recall from 26.3% to 38.5% but was not deployed. Test-set results are exploratory, because those partitions were used in earlier work. Nothing reported here was measured on children, school-board questions or other languages.
Keywords: automatic short-answer grading; selective prediction; sentence embeddings; natural language inference; unseen-question generalization
1. Introduction
Short-answer questions ask students to write a few words or sentences. Grading them by hand is slow, and different graders may disagree. Automatic short-answer grading (ASAG) has been studied for many years, using concept matching, information extraction, text similarity and supervised learning [1]. Word overlap alone is a weak grader. A correct answer can use different words from the reference answer, and a wrong answer can reuse the reference words while reversing their meaning. The SemEval-2013 student response analysis task treated grading as a problem of semantic inference, closely linked to recognizing textual entailment [2].
Two practical problems motivate this study. First, teachers want to add new questions without labelling new answers, so a grader must work on questions it has not seen. Scores on familiar questions can overstate this ability, and out-of-sample questions are a separate, harder test [3]. Second, a grader that can abstain and pass uncertain answers to a teacher changes what accuracy means. Accuracy on the accepted answers is meaningful only when reported with the share of answers accepted (coverage) and the accuracy over all answers [4], [5].
This work comes from a larger student project on a reading-skills profile for children, which has an oral-reading branch and a comprehension branch. This paper covers only the comprehension branch, as built and tested on public English data. It reports no oral-reading, speech or child-population results.
We ask four exploratory questions.
• RQ1. How does the three-signal scorer compare with simpler baselines and its own components?
• RQ2. How does performance differ between seen and unseen questions?
• RQ3. What accuracy–coverage trade-off appears when uncertain answers are sent for review?
• RQ4. What error patterns appear for partial-credit, negated, contradictory and irrelevant answers?
Contributions. (i) A documented description of a three-component scorer for three-level ASAG. (ii) A report of the gap between seen and unseen questions, with question counts beside each estimate. (iii) An evaluation of a fixed-threshold review rule, reporting selective accuracy with coverage and with overall accuracy, and showing that an 85% target was not met. (iv) An error analysis of partial-credit, negation and high-confidence failures. We do not claim a new algorithm. SBERT, NLI features, transformer classifiers, ensemble averaging and confidence thresholds are each established. The contribution is an honest account of how this combination behaves and where it fails.
Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 describes the scorer. Section 4 gives the experimental setup. Section 5 reports results, Section 6 discusses them, and Sections 7 and 8 give limitations and conclusions.
2. Related Work
ASAG surveys and the shared task. Burrows et al. organize ASAG research into eras based on concept mapping, information extraction, corpus-based methods and machine learning [1]. SemEval-2013 Task 7 supplied the SciEntsBank and Beetle corpora used here, with five-way, three-way and two-way labels [2].
Feature fusion. GradeAid combines lexical features with semantic features from a BERT-based model and feeds them to regressors [6]. As we understand its protocol, each question is handled in its own subset, so its results may not measure unseen-question performance [V11].
Transformers for ASAG. Camus and Filighera fine-tuned several pretrained transformers on SemEval-2013 data [7]. Ghavidel et al. applied BERT and XLNet to SciEntsBank [8]. Our DistilRoBERTa component belongs to this line of work, so transformer classification is not a contribution of this paper.
Sentence embeddings and unseen questions. Sentence-BERT gives sentence embeddings that can be compared directly [9]. Condor et al. used SBERT-based features and tested out-of-sample questions with leave-one-question-out protocols [3]. Our seen/unseen analysis has the same motivation but uses different data and a simpler split.
Pretrained encoders and inference. Our components build on RoBERTa [10], distilled models [11] and DeBERTaV3 [12]. NLI training data such as MultiNLI [13] is relevant because deciding whether a student answer agrees with or contradicts a reference answer resembles entailment.
Calibration and selective prediction. Modern neural networks are often poorly calibrated, and temperature scaling is a simple post-hoc fix [14]. Selective classification adds a rejection rule and describes performance as a trade-off between coverage and risk [4]. Traub et al. argue that a single working point is an incomplete evaluation and propose a multi-threshold summary [5]. We report coverage with every selective result, but only at fixed working points, because the full set of predictions needed for a risk–coverage curve has not been reconstructed (Section 5.3). QWK is a chance-corrected agreement measure that penalizes large disagreements more than small ones [15].
Comparability. Published scores are not directly comparable with ours, because label schemes, splits and metrics differ. Our three-level mapping is project-specific. We therefore make no numerical comparison with published systems.
3. Method
3.1 Task and labels
The scorer receives a question, a reference answer and a student answer, and predicts one ordered level: Incorrect (0), Partial (1) or Correct (2). Both datasets carry expert labels that the project collapsed to three levels (Table 1). This mapping is project-specific and is not the three-way task of SemEval-2013 [2]. QWK is computed only on the ordered three-level scale.

3.3 Combining the components
The deployed scorer takes an equal-weight average of the three components:
P_ens(y | x) = (1/3) · [P₁(y | x) + P₂(y | x) + P₃(y | x)]
Here x is the input, y a class and P₁–P₃ the component probabilities. Whether the average is taken over probabilities, logits or scores, and in which label space, must be confirmed from the inference code [V6]. Two ways of turning the average into a label were recorded. Path A collapses the averaged probabilities to three levels and takes the arg-max; it is the deployed path. Path B takes the five-way arg-max and then maps it to three levels; it was examined after the fact on development data and is not deployed. The two paths are never merged in this paper.
3.4 Confidence and the review rule
The confidence of a prediction is the largest of the three class probabilities. An answer is flagged for teacher review when confidence is below 0.64 [V4]. Answers at or above the threshold are scored automatically. The threshold was chosen from a risk–coverage table on development data, so it is tuned on the data used for development results. A temperature-scaling step was fitted with temperature 1.00, which leaves probabilities unchanged. The expected calibration error (ECE) was recorded as 0.017 [V4]. We therefore call the confidence the ensemble's maximum class probability, not a validated probability of being correct [14]. A contradiction-based review flag exists in the code but is switched off in the packaged policy.
3.5 What the scorer does not do
• It does not use rubric information for scoring. A demo can display rubric data, but the scoring path does not use it.
• It does not call a hosted model, API or large language model.
• A learned deferral model (gradient boosting) and question-embedding clustering were studied but are not deployed.
• It has not been tested on voice input, child speech or school-board questions.

We use the SciEntsBank and Beetle corpora from SemEval-2013 Task 7 [2]. Both contain English student answers to science questions, each with a reference answer and an expert label. SciEntsBank covers school science in 15 domains [18]. Beetle covers basic electricity and comes from a tutoring system [19].

4.2 Protocol and test-set status
Training epochs, ensemble choice and the 0.64 threshold were chosen on development data. The official test partitions were scored in earlier stages of the project, and the recorded access count is three. A full notebook run on 30 October 2026 also rewrote frozen files, although deployed scores were reproduced exactly and some older comparison tables moved by up to 0.006. Every test result in this paper is therefore exploratory and is not described as blind or independent. Development results are also optimistic, because choices were made on the same data and the deployed models used one training seed.
4.3 Metrics
• Overall accuracy: correct predictions ÷ all answers.
• Selective accuracy: correct predictions ÷ automatically accepted answers.
• Coverage: accepted answers ÷ all answers. Review rate: deferred answers ÷ all answers.
• QWK: on the ordered three-level labels only [15].
• Macro-F1: the label space (three or five) is stated in each table [V2].
Paired model comparisons used a question-level paired bootstrap with 1,000 resamples over the 177 development questions. The method for the QWK intervals was not recorded [V9]. No interval is available for selective accuracy, and no question-level macro-average of performance is available for the unseen questions. A non-overlapping interval is read only as an indication, not as a significance test.
4.4 Baselines
We compare against the majority-label predictor, TF-IDF similarity with logistic regression, the single fine-tuned DistilRoBERTa, a single RoBERTa-base fine-tune (weights not saved), an earlier simple average, and a variant with dataset-specific heads that needs the dataset identity at run time. Whether all comparisons use identical examples, label space and metric code is still to be confirmed [V10].
4.5 Provenance of reported figures
All figures come from the project's saved evaluation logs. In this draft they have not been recomputed from the saved UID-keyed prediction files [V3]. Developers' hard-coded values and a missing RoBERTa-base checkpoint limit full reproduction. A demo parity check matched the notebook on 15 of 15 reference cases (largest confidence difference 0.0005), but both call the same function, so this shows consistency and not independent replication. Hardware and software versions are not recorded here [V6].

6. Discussion
RQ1: comparison with baselines. The scorer clearly beats the majority label and TF-IDF with logistic regression in the development comparisons of Table 8. Against stronger models it shows no gain: on unseen development questions it is level with the best single fine-tuned model and with SBERT, and on exploratory test splits it is equal to or lower than an earlier simple average. No claim of general superiority or state-of-the-art performance is supported.
RQ2: seen and unseen questions. Accuracy falls from 69.2% on seen questions to 48.9% on unseen questions, and macro-F1 falls from 0.730 to 0.490 (Table 5). The seen and unseen QWK values show the same direction (0.630 and 0.317), but their aggregation path is unconfirmed [V1]. Three explanations are plausible and untested. The models may memorize wording from training questions. The unseen subset is dominated by a few questions with many answers, so a handful of questions can drive the pooled metrics. Reference answers may not describe what counts as an acceptable paraphrase. Because only 27 unseen questions are in the development subset, the gap is indicative. Per-question and question-level macro-averaged results are not yet available.
RQ3: accuracy–coverage trade-off. Deferral raises accuracy on accepted answers from 56.4% (all answers) to 75.1% on about 31% of development answers. The price is a review rate of about 69%, and about one in four accepted answers is still wrong. Reaching 85% needed coverage near 10% in-sample, so the 85%-at-30% target was not met. Whether this review load helps a teacher is a workflow question that these data cannot answer. The 0.64 threshold gives a usable ranking signal, but the confidence is the maximum class probability, not a validated probability of correctness, and the contradiction-based flag is switched off. A fair test of the threshold needs a question-grouped protocol with random-deferral and margin-based controls at matched coverage.
RQ4: error patterns. Partial-credit answers are the largest error class. Negation is handled unreliably in two small diagnostics. Some wrong answers are accepted silently above the threshold. Silent errors matter most in assessment, because they reach students without a teacher seeing them.
Evaluation choices change the picture. The choice of aggregation path moves Partial recall from 26.3% to 38.5%, and the label space changes macro-F1. Results must therefore be reported with their protocol. Prior work on per-question and leave-one-question-out grading [3], [6] addresses related but different questions, so we make no numerical comparison. We note only that our seen/unseen gap agrees in direction with the concern that motivates [3].
Not evidence for school or child use. The scorer was trained and tested on public English science data. Nothing was measured on children, CBSE or other school-board questions, Indian classroom answers, spoken answers or Telugu-native English learners. The system is an experimental assessment prototype, not a validated educational assessment tool.
7. Limitations
• Public English datasets do not establish performance on schoolchildren, CBSE assessments, other languages or the reading-skills system as a whole.
• The unseen development subset has only 27 questions, and about 63% of development answers come from them.
• The test partitions were accessed earlier (count three), so all test results are exploratory.
• Development results are optimistic: epochs, ensemble and the 0.64 threshold were chosen on the same data, with one training seed.
• The label scheme, the macro-F1 label space and the source of the seen/unseen QWK values are unresolved [V1], [V2].
• Interval methods are partly unrecorded. No selective-accuracy intervals and no question-level macro-averages exist.
• The threshold and learned deferral have not been tested in a leakage-safe, question-grouped protocol.
• The NLI contribution is unproven, and the negation diagnostics conflict.
• A cross-dataset transfer check was left out because its protocol could not be reproduced from the record.
• The scorer uses no rubric information. No human-grader agreement for the three-level scheme is available.
• Demo parity shows consistency, not independent replication, and some checkpoints are missing.
8. Conclusion and Future Work
We evaluated a three-component ensemble for three-level short-answer grading on SciEntsBank and Beetle. In development data it clearly beats simple lexical baselines. Its accuracy drops from 69.2% on seen questions to 48.9% on unseen questions, partial-credit answers cause most errors, and a fixed confidence threshold gives 75.1% accuracy on about 31% of answers, not the 85% targeted. Test figures are exploratory, and the values reported here are provisional until reproduced from saved predictions.
Future work, in priority order: (i) reproduce every figure from UID-keyed saved predictions and fix the label space; (ii) run a nested, question-grouped evaluation with a full risk–coverage analysis, a multi-threshold summary [5], and random and margin-based deferral controls; (iii) create a truly untouched test set before further selection; (iv) run a clean NLI ablation and improve negation and partial-credit handling; (v) test rubric-conditioned scoring; (vi) collect double-labelled, adjudicated data and report human agreement; (vii) build a teacher-adjudicated CBSE-style benchmark; and (viii) study child populations only with ethics approval, guardian consent, child assent and privacy safeguards.
